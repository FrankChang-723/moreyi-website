1. The Three Screening Criteria That Matter Most

Criterion 1 — Real manufacturing with IATF 16949

Brake pads are a safety component. The single best baseline proof that a supplier can make them consistently is IATF 16949, the automotive industry's quality management standard. It governs the whole production chain — incoming raw-material control, formula and batching discipline, pressing and curing processes, and outgoing inspection. Ask for the certificate, check the validity dates, and confirm the certified entity is the company you are actually paying.

Criterion 2 — Certifications matched to your market

A pad that is perfectly legal in one country can be unsellable in the next. Before comparing suppliers, write down your destination market's requirements (full checklist in Section 3) and require documented compliance for exactly those. "We can get it" is not the same as "we hold it" — Section 6 shows how that story usually ends.

Criterion 3 — Quality control you can audit

A reliable supplier can show you a closed loop: first-article samples sealed after composition analysis, 100% factory inspection on outgoing goods, and third-party inspection before loading. At MoreYi, our chain is: first-article sealed sample with SGS composition analysis → 100% outbound inspection → third-party pre-shipment inspection at AQL 2.5 before the container is sealed. If a supplier's "QC" is a photo of people in white coats, keep looking.

2. Brake Pad Quality Indicators: Know the Numbers Before You Negotiate

What friction coefficient should brake pads have?

For street use, most passenger-car pads run a friction coefficient (μ) between roughly 0.30 and 0.45. The peak number matters less than stability: a good pad holds its friction level across cold, wet and hot conditions instead of fading as the rotor heats up. The SAE J866 code printed on the pad edge or box (for example, "FF") grades the friction band — ask the supplier to explain theirs and back it with test data. If the only answer is "high friction, very good quality," you already have your answer.

What operating temperature range do brake pads need?

Typical reference ranges: NAO (organic) and ceramic street formulations are engineered to stay stable to roughly 350°C; semi-metallic formulas generally hold up to about 400–600°C. These are planning numbers — always require the supplier's test curve for the specific formulation you are buying, because two pads both labeled "ceramic" from two different factories can behave completely differently at 300°C.

What wear rate and mechanical properties are acceptable?

Wear rate is measured in standardized tests (SAE J2707 for friction material wear) — compare suppliers like-for-like on reports, not adjectives. Also require: shear strength per ISO 6312 (the friction material must not separate from the backing plate — separation is a catastrophic failure mode) and compressibility per ISO 6310 (what gives consistent pedal feel).

ECE R90 deserves a plain-language explanation, because it is the certification European buyers ask about most: R90 is the UN regulation for replacement brake components. A pad with R90 approval has been tested to perform within ±15% of the original-equipment pad it replaces, per approval family, at an accredited laboratory. Each approved part carries its E-mark and approval number.

And here is the nuance most buyers miss about "DOT": no US government agency issues a "DOT certificate" for brake pads. Compliance with FMVSS 105/135 is self-certified by the manufacturer, who must hold the test data on file. So when a supplier flashes an impressive-looking "DOT certificate," the correct response is: show me the FMVSS test reports and the manufacturer registration. Genuine suppliers have them ready.

How to verify a certificate is genuine — five steps

  1. Get the full identifiers — certificate number, issuing authority, issue and expiry dates.
  2. Match the name. The manufacturer name and factory address on the certificate must match the company you are paying. If the certificate belongs to some other company, your supplier is a middleman — or worse.
  3. Check the source. ECE type approvals are searchable in public registries; national approval authorities (for example Germany's KBA or the Netherlands' RDW) maintain databases you can query by approval number.
  4. Check the scope. Approvals cover specific part numbers or formulations. Make sure your SKU is inside the scope — not "similar to" it.
  5. Get the underlying test reports, including the laboratory's name. If the lab cannot be identified, neither can the quality.

4. Factory vs. Trading Company: 5 Verification Methods That Actually Work

"Why does it matter if the price is good?" — because when a quality problem appears eight months later, you need the people who actually control the formula, the press and the curing oven to answer for it. A trader can only forward your email. Here is how I verify anyone, and how buyers verify me:

Method 1 — Read the business license. Ask for the Chinese business license and read the business scope. A manufacturer's scope includes manufacturing/production of auto parts; a trader's says wholesale and import-export. The registration number can be checked against China's national registry.

Method 2 — Demand an unscripted video walk-through. Not the polished corporate video — a live call where you say "show me the mixing room now" and "open that curing oven." I've been in this trade 20 years; I can tell within two minutes of an unscripted walkthrough whether the person walking me around actually works in that building.

Method 3 — Ask questions only a maker can answer. What's your curing temperature curve? How do you prepare backing plates before bonding? What press tonnage runs this pad shape? A factory engineer will talk for ten minutes without stopping. A trader will change the subject to price. Buyers who ask these questions are my favorite buyers — they're also the hardest to disappoint.

Method 4 — Send a third-party auditor. SGS, TÜV and Bureau Veritas all offer affordable factory audits. Or simply make third-party pre-shipment inspection (AQL 2.5) a condition of your first order — we accept it as standard at MoreYi, before every container is sealed. A real factory has nothing to fear from a two-hour audit; a rented one collapses under it.

Method 5 — Trace the certificates. You are doing this anyway after Section 3. Certificates issued in a different company's name = middleman, confirmed.

6. Three Common Pitfalls (Real Scenarios, No Company Names)

Pitfall 1 — The low-price trap

A buyer receives a quote 25–30% below every other factory. Samples test fine. The first container is fine. By the third container, friction blocks are visibly thinner and the backing plates are a different, rougher steel — the supplier recovered the "discount" by swapping input costs, betting the buyer would never cut a pad open. When a price is dramatically below market, it is not a discount — it is a different product. Ask for a cost breakdown by material; a real manufacturer can produce one.

Pitfall 2 — The certificate that wasn't

A distributor is handed a clean-looking ECE R90 certificate PDF. A year later, a routine check shows the approval number belongs to a different factory's part family. The shipment is held, and the distributor spends months answering questions from the market authority. Five minutes on an approval registry before the first deposit would have caught it. That is why Section 3 exists — verify before you wire, not after it hurts.

Pitfall 3 — Batch instability

The first two orders pass inspection. The third batch squeals and wears out far faster. Nothing changed on paper — but the supplier quietly switched to a cheaper raw-material batch to protect margin when input prices rose. The defense is procedural, not personal: first-article sealed samples with composition analysis (we seal first articles after SGS composition analysis and check production batches against them), plus third-party pre-shipment inspection at AQL 2.5 on every container before it is sealed. Trust the process, and you can sleep while you're not there.
